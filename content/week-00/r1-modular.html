READING R1-P9Part 9 · Why do HW companies struggle to build AI software?—

Before the sessionsChris Lattner · 22 April 2025 · Original article

1. Main idea

Hardware companies spent more than ten years and billions of dollars on AI chips, but NVIDIA still leads. Lattner says the cause is structural: incentives, divided priorities and a low estimate of the software work. He calls this an “invisible matrix of constraints”.

2. Key points

  1. The pattern of the series: ambition, early excitement, then fragmentation. The CUDA moat gets deeper. A chip is not enough. A company needs a platform, and a platform needs long bets that do not always pay.
  2. Lattner describes his career in hardware and software together. At Intel he optimized launch software for the Pentium MMX. At Apple he built compiler infrastructure for the move to Apple’s own chips.
  3. At Google he scaled TPU software with the hardware and research teams. At SiFive he led engineering at a hardware company. His lesson: hardware and software teams speak different languages, move at different speeds and measure success differently.
  4. How hardware companies think. They design for the needs of two years ahead. In the CPU era, an LLVM back end gave a new chip a ready ecosystem. AI has no central compiler or operating system.
  5. Constraint 1: hardware companies do not see a software ecosystem as a product. Budgets, capex and tapeout dates dominate. The result is a demo-driven culture: a chip, a few kernels, some benchmarks and a keynote.
  6. Pressure 1: the treadmill of research. Workloads keep changing: Transformers, diffusion, MoEs, agents, new quantization. Each hardware feature adds software work to a large cross product of models, formats and targets.
  7. Pressure 2: a company competes with an industry, not only with CUDA. The open-source ecosystem optimizes for NVIDIA first. A new chip must beat the existing combination. This is software gravity (Part 3).
  8. Pressure 3: the software team is always outnumbered. It fights escalations and benchmark requests. Pressure 4: the business chases the whale, a large customer. Teams become consultants and make forks and tech debt.
  9. The regatta metaphor. Speedboats are startups. Racing yachts are single-chip companies and the largest tech companies, which build their own stacks. Ocean liners are Intel, AMD, Apple and Qualcomm. NVIDIA is the aircraft carrier.
  10. CUDA translators give compatibility, not speed. Intel’s oneAPI is open but lacks momentum. The article ends with the Matrix spoon scene: the constraints are a perception, and you must challenge the assumptions.

1. Main idea

Lattner says the problem is the complexity that CUDA drove into the whole stack, not CUDA itself. The fix needs a whole-system solution and years of focused work. He presents the plan of Modular: long closed research, then an open release.

2. Key points

  1. Promising efforts keep failing, and the stack gets heavier. Lattner praises the engineering of CUDA. But people resent it because it won. Real change is not small. It needs years of work by a large, focused team.
  2. What “democratizing” means to Lattner: change who can build what, and how, by removing gatekeepers. It is more than running on more devices. He gave a keynote in 2021 about a unifying software layer. Nobody finished it.
  3. He gives an analogy: Swift replaced Objective-C on the iPhone and widened the group of developers.
  4. He gives five success criteria. A stack must serve developers and unlock full hardware performance. It must allow innovation above and below the stack. It must scale across use cases and chips, and work in production. Lattner wants the design to be modular, like LLVM.
  5. Why do industry-scale changes fail? Not for lack of talent or money. Projects solve parts of the problem. Lattner uses the Lippitt-Knoster model, with six needs: vision, consensus, skills, incentives, resources and an action plan.

  8. The method: use your own platform as the first user. The test: “Can real engineers build real systems, faster, with this?” The bar rose through PyTorch, TorchScript and ONNX to TensorRT-LLM, vLLM and GenAI.
  9. A small team brought up the H100 from nothing. It ran real models in under two months. Lattner calls the speed comparable to existing stacks.
  10. Now the platform is open. After more than three years of closed work, Modular ships major releases every 6 to 8 weeks, with nightly builds. It open-sourced over half a million lines of GPU primitives, as the article states.

READING R1-P11Part 11 · How is Modular democratizing AI compute?—

Before the sessionsChris Lattner · 20 June 2025 · Original article

1. Main idea

Lattner says AI compute can only reach more people if the fragmented software is joined in one coherent system. The article presents the Modular Platform: Mojo, MAX and Mammoth, with open models and kernels. It scores the platform with a checklist.

2. Key points

  1. Fragmentation is a “disconnected starfield”. Modular wants a “constellation”: a coherent system with a map. The cause is “dark matter”: vendor silos, siloed software stacks and old abstractions.
  2. CUDA and OpenCL come from before the GenAI era. CUDA started the AI revolution, but GenAI needs a stack designed for it. No single hardware vendor can unify the field, because vendors build for their own chips.
  3. The goal is one programmable system that scales across vendors and performs well on the most used GPUs and CPUs. The article says one “small binary” can run on many hardware types.
  4. The layers work with existing tools such as PyTorch, vLLM and CUDA. A developer can enter at the layer that fits the need. See Figure 0-3.
  5. Mojo is the layer closest to the hardware. It has Python-like syntax and aims for the speed of C++, Rust and CUDA. Mojo files sit beside Python modules without bindings. It can use Tensor Cores and TMAs.
  6. MAX is the modeling and serving layer, with a PyTorch-like Python API. It covers KV-cache lifecycles, paged attention, speculative decoding and hardware-aware scheduling. The container is about 1 GB.
  7. The article claims that one MAX binary runs on H200, B200 and MI325X. Support for MI355 and B300 is planned. It also runs on mixed CPU and GPU systems.
  8. Mammoth is the cluster layer. It is Kubernetes-native. It turns fixed GPU capacity, on-premises or in the cloud, into an elastic inference fabric. It splits prefill and decode across GPUs and schedules many vendors as one pool.
  9. Models and kernels are open source in the modular/modular repository. The kernels are written in Mojo. Researchers can start with proven parts and go deeper.
  10. Hardware diversity is a strength. Portability across NVIDIA and AMD is shown. Modular wants more hardware partners. ASIC support is “designed for” but not yet proven.
  11. The checklist has twelve criteria from the series. The article marks most as achieved and ASIC support as partial. The next part will cover Mojo and xPU programmability.
