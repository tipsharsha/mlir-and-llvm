RD-1Generated code versus hand-written kernelsHexagon NPU

1. Question

When does compiler-generated NPU code match a hand-written kernel? Where must the line between the two be?

2. Evidence

  • Hexagon-MLIR makes kernels from Triton and PyTorch. The 2025 slides report up to 80 percent of hand-written speed in early results.
  • The ModCon post describes hand-written Mojo kernels for GEMM, decode, LayerNorm, softmax and attention.
  • Part 7 reports a gap of about 20 percent for Triton on an NVIDIA GPU. The same debate exists there.

3. Why it is open

The public sources give no comparison of the two paths on the same kernels, shapes and chip.

4. A first study

Select three kernels, for example GELU, RMS-norm and attention. Measure both paths. Report speed, code size and the effort to write each version.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 4 to 6 (MLIR), Weeks 7 to 9 (Mojo) and Week 12 (MAX). It is also the fusion question for the sessions.

RD-2Cost models for tiling and schedulingHexagon NPU

1. Question

How does a compiler choose tile sizes, thread counts and buffer depths for an NPU with a scratchpad memory?

2. Evidence

  • In the paper, multithreaded GELU is 2.28 times faster at 32K elements and 3.95 times faster at 512K. It is slower than one thread below about 16K elements.
  • The speedup falls to 3.40 times at 1M elements. The authors link this to pressure on TCM and the DMA queues.
  • The paper mentions a polytope-size heuristic and asks for better scheduling heuristics. It gives no details.
  • The paper’s idealized model for double buffering gives a speedup of 1/max(m, 1−m). The benefit is zero when a kernel is only memory-bound or only compute-bound.

3. Why it is open

The heuristics are not described. Real kernels fall between the two extremes of the model, so a simple rule does not hold.

4. A first study

Build an analytical model from DMA bandwidth, HVX throughput and thread start-up cost. Compare it with measurements. Then test a search method or a learned model.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 4 to 6. Use the tiling and bufferization passes of MLIR.

RD-3Operations that need more than one genericCPU first

1. Question

Which MLIR abstractions let a compiler fuse and tile softmax, scan and TopK?

2. Evidence

  • The paper says that softmax, prefix-sum and TopK cannot be one linalg.generic. They need control-flow operations.
  • The paper reports 4.7 times for flash attention in float32. Attention is an important case for LLMs.

3. Why it is open

A structured operation for scan or sort is not part of the core set that the paper uses. Fusing such operations is hard.

4. A first study

Study how upstream MLIR represents reductions, scans and sorts. On a CPU, prototype an online-softmax fusion. Check if tiling still works.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 4 to 6. This direction needs no NPU hardware.

RD-4Compiler-generated matrix-engine codeHexagon NPU

1. Question

Can the compiler make HMX-level GEMM from linalg and a description of the layout?

2. Evidence

  • The paper sends matrix multiplications and convolutions to a matrix engine. It handles layout with linalg.pack and linalg.unpack.
  • The paper does not list HMX work as future work.
  • In the ModCon post, the GEMM on HMX is a hand-written Mojo kernel.

3. Why it is open

The public sources do not give the HMX tile and layout rules. They do not compare generated and hand-written GEMM.

4. A first study

Write down the HMX layout rules from the Hexagon documents. Express them as pack and unpack layouts. Compare the generated GEMM with the hand-written one.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 4 to 6 (layouts and lowering) and Weeks 10 and 11 (tiled matrix multiplication).

RD-5Low-precision formatsPartly CPU

1. Question

How must a compiler handle block-scaled formats and native float16 math on a vector unit?

2. Evidence

  • In the paper, SiLU in float16 is 4.8 times faster, and in float32 it is 7.1 times faster. The authors link the loss to internal promotion to float32.
  • The paper uses polynomial approximations (Taylor series with Horner’s method) for functions such as exp.
  • The ModCon post mentions MXFP6 weights and an int8 KV cache.

3. Why it is open

The paper describes no quantization pass. The public sources give no error analysis of the approximations.

4. A first study

Build native float16 approximations with proven error bounds. Test them against a reference in units of last place (ULP). Study this on a CPU first.

5. Links to your program

Week 1 (floating-point and -ffast-math) and Week 8 (SIMD in Mojo).

RD-6LLM decode and communicationCloud AI 100

1. Question

How can a compiler overlap communication with compute during LLM decode on a card with several SoCs?

2. Evidence

  • The ModCon post runs Gemma 4 31B across four SoCs with tensor parallelism.
  • The post lists the cost of all-reduce during decode as remaining work.
  • The post says that QCCL does not yet support in-kernel communication. An expert-parallel MoE model must wait.

3. Why it is open

Decode is small and latency-bound. Each all-reduce is a large part of each step.

4. A first study

Model tensor-parallel decode with the MLIR sharding dialect. It had the name mesh in older releases, so check your version. Estimate how much overlap is possible.

5. Links to your program

Week 12 (MAX) and the questions about GPU and card targets in the sessions.

RD-7Dynamic shapes and compile timePartly CPU

1. Question

How can a compiler specialize kernels for changing sequence lengths without long compile times?

2. Evidence

  • The paper does not mention dynamic shapes.
  • Part 6 says that auto-tuning made the compiles of TVM slow.
  • The ModCon post says that the kernels use the QAIC JIT API, so compile time is part of the run.

3. Why it is open

The sequence length changes at each decode step. This is my inference. The sources do not discuss it.

4. A first study

Measure compile time against the number of kernel variants. Compare bucketing of lengths with symbolic shapes.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 7 to 9 (parameters in Mojo are compile-time) and Week 12.

RD-8Hardware and compiler co-designInternal data

1. Question

Which compiler-visible features of a future NPU give the most speed for LLM kernels?

2. Evidence

  • Part 9 says that hardware companies struggle to build AI software. Qualcomm builds both the chips and, now, the Modular software.
  • The ModCon post names the AI200, AI250 and AI300 as the roadmap.
  • The post says that the next version of DeviceContext lets hardware companies do their own backend bring-up.

3. Why it is open

Outside groups cannot change the hardware. The effect of scratchpad size, DMA queues or matrix tile shape is not public.

4. A first study

Make the cost model of RD-2 depend on hardware parameters. Sweep the parameters and rank them by effect on LLM kernels.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 10 and 11. Check the rules of Qualcomm before you use internal parameters.

RD-9Vectorization in the LLVM Hexagon backendHexagon toolchain

1. Question

Can the cost model of the loop vectorizer improve for 128-byte vectors and VLIW packets?

2. Evidence

  • The 2025 slides give HVX vectors of 128 bytes.
  • The ModCon post says that Mojo compiles to the Hexagon LLVM NPU backend.
  • Week 1 shows how to read the vectorizer remarks with -pass-remarks.

3. Why it is open

The sources do not say how good the vectorizer decisions are on Hexagon. This is a classic LLVM problem, so it is a safe place to start.

4. A first study

Collect loops where the measured best choice is not the choice of the vectorizer. Tune the cost model on them. Check that other loops do not get slower.

5. Links to your program

Weeks 1 to 3 (LLVM IR and passes).
